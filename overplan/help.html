A scan you can correct

Help

Overplan · Last updated 10 August 2026

Overplan turns a room into a plan you can correct. Scan it if your device has LiDAR, draw it by hand if it does not, or trace a plan you already have — then fix what the scanner got wrong, which is the part most scanning apps leave you stuck with. Every diagram on this page is a drawn glyph, not a screenshot — it describes the gesture, not one particular button's position, so it stays right as the app's own layout keeps changing.

Starting a plan

Scan a room

Scan a room uses your device's LiDAR camera to measure the walls as you walk the room, and needs camera access to do it. It needs a device with a LiDAR sensor: on a device without one, tapping it says so rather than opening a scanner that cannot work, and the two rows below it work on every device.

A scan is a first draft, not a verdict. Every wall it measures can be moved, every opening repositioned, and the room's height adjusted, once you're in the 3D view — see The 3D view and Editing what you've added.

Sketch a floorplan

Sketch a floorplan works on every device, LiDAR or not: tap out the corners and the walls stand up on their own.

Tap to place a corner Two fingers pan Pinch to zoom Fit re-frames the drawing

Tap once to place the first corner, then tap again to set the far end — that draws your first wall. Keep tapping to add the next one, and the next: each tap adds another wall to the chain. Tap a corner you've already placed to close the loop and finish the room.

One finger is reserved for placing corners, so panning and zooming both use two fingers — drag with two fingers to slide the page, or pinch to zoom, at any point while you're drawing. If a pan or a zoom carries the drawing off-screen, the Fit button brings the whole thing back into view; it always works, even on a blank canvas.

The Sketch Settings button on the canvas sets the snap spacing, the major grid band, and the thickness the walls will be given when the drawing becomes a room.

Trace an imported plan

Trace an imported plan is the third way to start: a photo of a paper plan, a floor plan PDF from an agent, a scan of a hand sketch. Pick the file and it drops straight onto the sketch canvas underneath your drawing, at a rough guessed size — then you set its scale and line it up while you trace. See Tracing an imported plan for all of its settings.

The sample room

Overplan ships with a sample room — a real, finished, furnished plan — and copies it into your library the first time you open the app, so there is something to orbit, measure and share before you have scanned anything. It is an ordinary plan from that moment on: it opens, edits, renames, exports and deletes like any other.

If you delete it or edit it beyond recognition, Restore Sample Room in the ••• menu on the Plans screen brings the original back. When a plan called Sample Room is still in your library, it asks first — Replace discards your changes to it, Keep Both adds a fresh copy alongside what you have.

The 3D view

Getting around

Once a room exists — scanned, sketched or traced — it opens in the 3D view, where every edit in this help file happens. Looking around it uses one finger; changing what you're looking at uses two.

One finger orbits Two fingers pan Pinch to zoom Two-finger double-tap resets

Drag with one finger to orbit around the room. Drag with two fingers to slide the room across the screen instead — the same two fingers can pinch to zoom at the same time, so panning and zooming blend into one motion, the way it works in Maps.

Lost your place after a wide orbit or an over-eager zoom? Double-tap with two fingers and the view snaps back — recentred and re-fit to the room, keeping the angle you were looking from.

Walls, doors and windows

Adding a wall, door or window

Add opens a small palette with three choices: Wall, Door, Window.

Wall Door Window Tap the wall it goes on

Door or Window — tap the one you want, then tap the wall where it goes. It appears already placed and already selected, at whatever size you used last, ready to move or resize right away. Tapping a spot the opening cannot fit keeps it armed and shows why, so your very next tap is the retry.

Wall — a new interior wall has to start from an existing one, so tapping Wall first asks you to tap the wall it should attach to. From there, drag out its length and direction: it snaps to existing corners, to running perpendicular, and to 45° steps, with a confirmation showing the length before it commits.

Editing what you've added

Tap to select Drag to move Drag an edge to resize

Tap a wall, a door, a window, or the floor or ceiling, to select it. Tap empty space, or tap the same thing again, to deselect it. Only one thing is selected at a time.

  • A selected wall moves when you drag it. It cannot be resized directly — a wall's length follows from the corners at each end, which is what Outline mode is for.
  • A selected door or window slides along its wall when you drag its middle, and resizes when you drag one of its edges.
  • The selected floor or ceiling changes the room's height when you drag it.

Whatever is selected shows its own card with the actions that apply to it — including Delete; see Deleting.

The move grip on a wall

Select a wall and an amber dot with a double-headed arrow appears at its middle. It is a sign, not a control you have to use: dragging anywhere on a selected wall has always moved it, and the grip is there so you can see that before you try. The arrow points along the axis the wall actually travels, which is the same axis your drag is measured on.

A wall the plan cannot move — one whose corners are pinned by the walls around it — gets no grip. Dragging it still tells you why rather than doing nothing.

Outline mode

Outline (in the dock) is the fast way to fix a room's shape: it opens the room's own corners on a flat 2D canvas, the same one Sketch uses.

  • Tap a corner, then tap it again to drag it into place.
  • Tap a wall, then tap it again to slide it — or to delete it. A corner itself can't be deleted directly; delete the walls that meet at it instead.
  • Draw a brand-new wall off a selected corner or wall, or straight onto empty ground, the same way you would in a fresh sketch.

The first press only highlights, deliberately — it is what stops a stray finger dragging your room out of shape. Done writes your changes back onto this same room. Cancel only asks first if something has actually changed since you opened it.

Deleting

A wall or an opening: select it, then use Delete on its own card. A single undo is offered immediately afterwards, right on screen.

A whole plan: its menu in Plans, then Delete. This one is confirmed before it happens, because there is no undo for an entire plan the way there is for one wall.

Measuring

Taking a measurement

Measure (in the dock) replaces the dock with measure mode's own controls. Tap two points and Overplan snaps each one to the nearest real feature — a corner, an edge, a wall face — and names what it snapped to, so a dimension is never taken from a point that does not actually exist on the room.

Tap a point Tap a second point

Wall to wall specifically measures between the clear, finished faces of two walls — the number a tape would give you, not a centre-line distance. Two walls that have been dragged out of parallel have no single clear width, so that pair is refused with a sentence rather than given a number that would not mean anything.

Every measurement attaches to the room, not to the screen, so it follows your edits: move a wall and the numbers change with it.

The Wall, Edge and Corner filters

Measure mode's controls are three switches and a way out: Wall · Edge · Corner · Back. Each switch decides whether the aim is allowed to land on that kind of feature at all. They light up when they are on, so what you are aiming at is readable at a glance instead of being something you find out by tapping.

  • Wall — flat faces. Turn Edge and Corner off and every tap lands on a face, which is what you want when you are dimensioning wall to wall across a room full of corners.
  • Edge — the lines where two surfaces meet.
  • Corner — single points. The most precise of the three, and the one that otherwise wins whenever your finger is near one.

At least one has to stay on: with all three off, no tap could resolve anything. Turning off the last one gives a light tick and declines, rather than leaving you in a mode that ignores you. Your choice is remembered between plans and between launches, because "I am dimensioning wall to wall today" is a fact about how you are working, not about one room.

Back leaves measure mode. So does a tap on clear ground when you have nothing half-measured.

The Measurements list

Measurements — the chip beside the dock while measure mode is on — lists every measurement in the room: what each one is between, what it currently reads, and a pencil to caption it or a bin to delete it.

It exists because the room itself cannot show you all of them: a measurement on the far side of the plan, or one whose ends have orbited out of sight, has nothing on screen at all. The list is also where a refused pair explains itself.

The letters down the left match the register on the PDF drawing, in the same order, and the captions you type here are the captions that print on it.

Tracing an imported plan

Bringing in a photo or a PDF

Trace an imported plan, on the Plans screen, opens the file picker for a PNG, JPEG or HEIC photo or a PDF — a photo of a paper plan taken on your iPhone is a HEIC, and works. For a PDF, the first page is used; there is no page picker.

There is no calibration screen in the way. The image lands on the sketch canvas straight away, at a guess of about 7 m along its longer side, and you correct that guess afterwards while you can actually see it against the grid. Everything you draw is your own drawing — the image underneath is a reference and never becomes part of the plan.

An imported image has no geometry of its own, so your corners snap to the grid over it, not to lines in the picture. That is the difference between tracing an image and tracing a scan, where there are real corners to land on.

Image scale

Open Sketch Settings on the canvas and Image scale is the first control. It is one number: how long the image's longer side is in the real world. Nudge it in 10 cm steps with the stepper, or tap the number itself to type one.

The way to set it is to find something in the picture whose length you know — a door, a run of wall, a dimension printed on the plan — and adjust until that thing reads about right against the grid. A room is usually 5–10 m across, which is where the opening guess comes from.

Moving it and pinning it

Position on the grid… hands the canvas over to moving the image. The toolbar is replaced by an accent-coloured bar reading Moving the image, the image gets a dashed outline, and it is forced visible — so there is no doubt about which layer your finger has hold of. Two fingers still pan and zoom throughout.

Drag it roughly there Pin a point exactly

Dragging gets it roughly right, landing on the same snap grid your drawing uses. Pin a Point makes it exact, in two taps: tap something on the image you can recognise — a corner of the traced plan — then tap where that point belongs on the grid, and it lands exactly there.

Back on the panel, Move across and Move up are the same position as two typed numbers, stepping by your current snap spacing — for when you already know the template is 40 cm too far east. They read as an offset because a freshly imported image starts centred on the plan's origin. Done leaves position mode.

Rotate, opacity and mirror

Rotate Image underlay Mirror image

Rotate turns the image, one degree a tap or a typed number, anywhere in −180° to 180° — for squaring a photographed plan up against the drawing grid.

Image underlay is the opacity slider: how faintly the reference shows through. At 0% it is hidden, and hidden means it also stops being snapped to, which is worth knowing before you slide it down to get a clearer look at your own lines.

Mirror image flips it left to right, for a plan that was photographed or exported the wrong way round.

Trace mode, over a room you already have

Trace (in the dock, and only offered once a room has walls) is the same canvas pointed at a different reference: it starts a brand-new drawing with your current room shown faintly underneath it. Because the room is real geometry, your new corners snap onto its own corners and walls, so a hand-drawn trace can land exactly where the original says it should.

The difference from Outline is what each one touches: Outline edits the room you already have; Trace draws a separate, brand-new one and leaves the original completely untouched. Reach for Trace when you want a second version to compare, rather than a correction to the one you've got.

The opacity slider is the same one, named Scan underlay here, with the same rule: at 0% the reference is neither shown nor snapped to.

Your plans, and Files

Plans, and duplicating one

Plans is where every scan, sketch and trace you've made lives, one card each. Tap a card to open it. The ••• on a card is its own menu, ordered by consequence — safest first, destructive last: Open .overplan from Files, Rename, Duplicate, Share, View in AR, then Restore, then Delete.

Duplicate copies a plan whole, including the original scan or drawing it started from — so you can take a room in two different directions and still be able to restore either copy back to the same starting point on its own. Duplicating is never confirmed, because it only ever adds a plan; the copy is one menu away from being deleted again if you change your mind.

The .overplan file

.overplan is Overplan's own document format, and it is the plan itself rather than a picture of one. It carries the room, the original scan or drawing, your edits, your openings, your furniture and your measurements — so it opens back up on another device fully editable, exactly as you left it. It is also what to keep as a backup. Every other export is a rendering: a PDF or a 3D model can be looked at and printed and measured, but it cannot be re-opened here and carried on with.

One plan is one file. Nothing about it needs an account, a login or a server.

Your plans are in Files

Overplan keeps its plans where you can see them: open the Files app and look under On My iPhone (or On My iPad) → Overplan. Every plan is one .overplan file with its own icon, and you can copy, move, rename, organise into folders, AirDrop or back them up from there like any other document.

Plans stay on your device. Overplan has no account, no cloud and no analytics; nothing leaves the device unless you share it. If you want a plan in iCloud Drive or on a computer, copy it there yourself — that is a decision you make, not a default.

Opening a plan from somewhere else

A .overplan from another device — AirDropped, downloaded, sitting in iCloud Drive or on a shared drive — opens in two ways:

  • From Overplan: ••• on any card in Plans → Open .overplan from Files…, then pick the file. The picker greys out anything that is not a plan.
  • From outside: tap or share the file — in Files, Mail, Messages, or straight off an AirDrop — and choose Overplan. It is imported as a plan of your own and appears as a new card.

If a file turns out not to be a readable plan, Overplan says which of the three things went wrong — not a plan at all, written by a newer version of Overplan than this one, or incomplete — because the three need different actions from you.

Restoring the original

Every plan keeps its starting point untouched, separately from whatever you've edited since — whichever way it began. The button is named for what it actually goes back to: Restore Original Scan for a room that started as a scan, or Restore Drawn Outline for one that started as a sketch or a trace.

Either one discards every edit made since — walls moved, openings added or removed, measurements taken — keeping only what was originally measured or drawn. It asks for confirmation first, because there is no further undo once you confirm. Reach it from a plan's menu in Plans, or from Room inside the editor itself.

A room saved by an older version of Overplan, before this kept a separate original, shows why the button is unavailable rather than just greying it out with no explanation.

Sharing

The Share screen

Share — the share button in the editor's toolbar, or Share… from a card's ••• menu in Plans — is one screen with every way out of Overplan on it, in four groups: View in AR, then Overplan, 2D PDF and 3D model.

Nothing leaves your device until you pick one and choose a destination.

View in AR — pinch to shrink it

View in AR is the first row, on its own, because it is not an export: it opens your room in the room you are standing in, and hands over no file at all. Tap it, then tap AR in the viewer.

It opens at real size. A full-size building appears around you, so if you are indoors the first thing you see is likely to be a wall in your face, with nothing on screen saying it can be changed. Pinch to shrink it until it is a model on the table in front of you. You can also drag it to reposition it and twist to rotate it. This surprises everybody once; it is the viewer working as intended, not a fault.

The first tap takes a moment while the model is prepared. Sharing the same room as USDZ afterwards is instant, because it is the same file.

On a device that cannot place a model in the room around it, the row reads View in 3D instead and says so — you still get the model, just without the AR tab.

Overplan — Project package

Project package (.overplan) shares the complete, editable project — for another device, a desktop, or a backup. It is the one option that hands over the plan rather than a picture of it: whoever receives it can open it in Overplan and keep working.

It shares a copy, and any edit still in flight is written first, so what goes out is always what you are looking at. The row is unavailable for a room that has never been saved, because there is no file yet — save it first.

2D PDF — three documents

  • Drawing — a printable, to-scale 2D floor plan with dimensions. Pick a paper size and the button shows the scale it prints at; a size too small for the room stays on screen marked "won't fit" rather than disappearing, because that is itself useful information about the room. Your captioned measurements print in its register.
  • True scale (1:1) — the plan drawing at real size, for CAD use. No frame, no title block, no register, no dimensions: the geometry, on a page the size of the drawing itself. A CAD or vector tool opening it gets real paths at real dimensions.
  • Presentation — a rendered overhead view, for showing the result. Not to scale. It looks like a member of the same family as the Drawing and means the opposite thing: the Drawing is meant to be measured, this one must never be.

Both of the first two are previewed before they are shared, so you can see the page before anyone else does.

3D model — USDZ, GLB, OBJ

  • USDZ — Apple's AR/Quick Look format. Best for previewing on iPhone, iPad or Mac and sharing with Apple apps. Send one to somebody and they can walk your plan at full size with nothing to install.
  • GLB — glTF binary. Widely supported by web and game tools.
  • OBJ — universal text format. Opens in nearly every 3D tool. It is the oldest and plainest of the three: it comes with a companion .mtl file for the colours (share both together), and unlike USDZ and GLB it carries no baked shading, so it arrives flatter and is lit entirely by whatever you open it in.

All three include the furniture you've placed when Furniture visible is on in the view settings, and leave it out when it is off — the model matches what you are looking at.

Getting a 3D file onto a computer

For Blender, or any desktop 3D tool, the route is the ordinary one — the file is a normal file, so use whatever you already use to move files:

  • AirDrop to a Mac. Pick the format, then AirDrop from the share sheet; it lands in Downloads.
  • Save to Files — into iCloud Drive, or into any storage provider you have in Files — then open it on the computer from the same place. This works for Windows too, via iCloud for Windows or any provider you both can see.
  • Mail it to yourself, which is often the shortest path for one file.

Blender imports all three formats. USDZ and GLB carry the baked shading and their colours in one file; OBJ needs its .mtl companion kept alongside it. If a model imports looking oddly tiny or enormous, check the import scale in the tool rather than re-exporting — the file states its own units.

To carry on editing the plan rather than the mesh, send the .overplan instead. A 3D model is a one-way trip out of Overplan.

Two tips worth knowing

If snapping is fiddly, zoom in

Snapping works to a fixed distance on the glass — about a fingertip — never to a distance in the room. That is deliberate: the reach of your finger does not change when you zoom, so a gesture you learn at one zoom keeps working at every other.

The useful consequence is that zooming in spreads the features apart under your finger. A corner and an edge that were fighting over the same fingertip at whole-room framing are centimetres apart when you are close, and the tap goes exactly where you meant. When a snap keeps grabbing the wrong thing, or a corner will not go quite where you want it, zoom closer first — it works better than aiming harder.

Tap the ground to back out

Whenever you are somewhere you did not mean to be — something selected, a door armed, a palette open, measure mode running — tap the ground, clearly outside the building's footprint. It does exactly what that level's own Back, Cancel or Done button does.

One tap goes back one level, never two, so you can tap your way out without losing more than you meant to. It has to be clear ground, not a patch of floor inside the room: a tap on the floor is a tap on the room, and closing things out from under somebody who was only looking at their plan would be worse than not offering the shortcut. The proper buttons are always there too, for when you are zoomed in far enough that no ground is on screen.

If something looks wrong

Scanning shows a black screen, or nothing happens

Camera access is off. Open iOS Settings → Overplan → Camera, or use the Open Settings button in the app's own alert. Scanning uses the camera and LiDAR to measure the room and records nothing.

It says this device can't scan

Room scanning needs a LiDAR sensor, which only some iPhone and iPad models have, and no setting anywhere turns one on. Sketch a floorplan and Trace an imported plan work on every device and reach the same editor and the same kind of plan.

The room looks flat when it first opens

Shading is computed after the plan appears and settles a moment later. This is normal.

In AR I'm inside a wall

The model opened at real size, which is what it is for — and indoors that means the building is around you. Pinch to shrink it to a tabletop model, then drag it where you want it. See View in AR.

Trace is greyed out

There's no room yet to trace over. Scan or sketch one first — or use Trace an imported plan from the Plans screen, which brings its own reference with it.

A measurement looks wrong

Check which feature it snapped to — the label says. Dimensions are taken between wall faces, not centre lines. If it keeps snapping to the wrong kind of thing, turn that kind off in the Wall / Edge / Corner switches, and zoom in.

Restore is disabled

The room was saved by an earlier version of Overplan, which didn't keep a separate original to go back to. Anything scanned, sketched or traced from now on keeps one.
